# A Kid's Guide to This Project

Hi! This page explains a website I built. You don't need to know anything about computers to read this. If you can read a story, you can read this. Ready? Let's go.

---

## What is this thing?

Imagine a giant chalkboard at the front of a classroom. Every minute, a friendly robot writes new numbers on the chalkboard. The numbers tell people something useful — like which fruit is cheap today, and which is expensive.

This project is exactly like that chalkboard, except:

- The chalkboard is a **website** (a page on the internet).
- The robot is a **computer program**.
- The numbers are about **money in Nigeria** — specifically, how Nigerians swap one kind of money for another.

The chalkboard is for one special reader: a company called **Quidax**. Quidax is a place online where Nigerians can buy and sell different kinds of money safely. I built this website to show Quidax a fun, important idea I had about their business.

---

## What is "money in Nigeria"?

Most countries have their own money. In Nigeria, the money is called the **naira**, and the symbol looks like this: ₦.

But there's another kind of money called the **dollar** ($), used by lots of countries to pay for things from each other. To buy things from another country, Nigerians have to swap nairas for dollars first.

There's also a special kind of digital money called a **stablecoin**. The most popular one is called **USDT**. One USDT is supposed to always be worth one dollar, like a dollar's digital twin.

So in Nigeria, lots of people swap nairas for USDT, because USDT is easier to send across the internet than real dollars.

## What does my website show?

It shows three things, updated live:

1. **How much it costs** to swap nairas for USDT right now, on a website called Quidax.
2. **How that price compares** to the price the Nigerian government says it should be.
3. **A big plan I have** for how Quidax could make a lot more money.

It also has pretty charts and tables, spread across **six different pages** you can click between — like rooms in a house.

## What's inside the website?

Let's walk through it room by room, like a house tour.

### Room 1: The front door (the "Hero")

When you open the website, the first thing you see is a big greeting. It's like the cover of a book. It says, in big letters, what the website is for. It also has a little glowing dot that says **"live"** — that means the numbers really are fresh.

### Room 2: The Key Claims

Right after the front door, there's a sign with **5 things I want Quidax to know**. Like:

- "Quidax is the cheapest place to swap nairas for USDT in Nigeria."
- "There's a chance to earn lots more money by helping other companies, not just regular people."

Each of these is like a chapter title. If you want to know more, you can scroll down to find proof.

### Room 3: The Big Numbers (KPIs)

Now we get to a wall of **four giant numbers**. KPI is short for "Key Performance Indicator," which is fancy talk for "a number that really, really matters."

The numbers are things like:

- How many nairas changed hands today.
- How much of that was for USDT (the digital dollar).
- How many different pairs of money Quidax lets you swap.
- How close Quidax's price is to the official price.

These are like the scoreboard at a soccer game.

### Room 4: The Premium Picture

Next is a chart showing **three prices side by side**:

- The price Quidax sells USDT for.
- The price the Nigerian government says is correct (called "NFEM").
- The price you'd get in the street market (called "parallel").

If Quidax's price is close to the government price, that's a good sign — it means Quidax is fair.

### Room 5: The Peg Watch

There's a special coin called **cNGN**. It's another stablecoin, but instead of being worth one dollar, one cNGN is supposed to be worth one naira. Always. Forever.

But sometimes it isn't! Sometimes a cNGN might be worth ₦0.98 or ₦1.02 instead. That's called a **"depeg"** — like a horse breaking free from its post.

This room watches the cNGN like a hawk and shouts if it ever escapes its post:

- **Green** = stable, all good.
- **Yellow** = wobbling, keep an eye on it.
- **Red** = depeg! Something's wrong!

### Room 6: The Stablecoin Deep-Dive

This room has a fancy line graph showing how the USDT/naira price has moved over the past few days. There's also a pie chart breaking down what kinds of digital dollar things Nigerians use most.

### Room 7: The Competitive Matrix

Quidax isn't the only company doing this. There are others: Yellow Card, Busha, Luno, Roqqu. This is a **table** comparing all of them, side by side, like a Pokémon stats card.

For each company we list:

- How many countries it works in.
- How many different money pairs it offers.
- Whether it has a license from the Nigerian government.

And so on. Quidax is the row colored a tiny bit differently — because the website is *for* Quidax.

### Room 8: The B2B Strip

"B2B" is a grown-up word meaning **business to business**. Most websites that sell digital dollars sell to regular people. But some sell to **other companies** instead — those companies then use the digital dollars to do their own business.

This little strip shows the companies that *only* do B2B, like Conduit, Bitnob, and Yellow Card. They're not on the big matrix because they're a different shape, but I list them so nobody can say "you forgot about them."

### Room 9: The Corridor Cards

A **corridor** is a money path between two countries. Nigeria sends a lot of money to China to buy stuff. That's a corridor: NG → CN.

Each card shows:

- How much money flows through that corridor every year (like, $13 billion for Nigeria → China!).
- How long it takes the slow old way (with banks).
- How long it takes the fast new way (with stablecoins).
- How much money is saved.

The fast new way is *much* faster. Like, minutes vs. days. That's why digital dollars are exciting.

### Room 10: The B2B Sliders

This is the most fun room. There are **little sliders**, like volume knobs on a stereo, and you can drag them. As you drag them, a big number at the top changes.

The big number is **how much money I think Quidax could make** if they tried hard at B2B. The sliders let you change my guesses — like, "what if Quidax captures only 1% of the market?" or "what if they capture 3%?"

If you drag a slider too far past what's realistic, a **warning** pops up. It says, "Hey, you're outside the realistic range — this number isn't believable anymore." That's so nobody can take a screenshot of a crazy big number and pretend I said it.

### Room 11: Customer Proof

Three companies — **Basqet**, **Blano**, and **Gigxpad** — already use Quidax's tools. Their logos and names are on cards. This proves Quidax's B2B idea isn't just an idea; it's already happening, just quietly.

### Room 12: Recommendations

Now I give Quidax **specific advice**: "If you do these things, you'll be in great shape." Like: "Make a special product called 'Quidax Treasury API'." Or: "Publish how reliable you are so businesses trust you."

Each piece of advice has a little note saying **"I might be wrong about this — they may already do it internally."** I added that note so Quidax doesn't get mad if they're already doing it.

### Room 13: Counter-Thesis

This room is brave. It's where I say **"here's how I could be wrong."** I list four things that would prove my whole big idea wrong, and I tell Quidax exactly which numbers on the dashboard would warn them first.

Most people writing pitches hide their weaknesses. I show them on purpose. It makes the rest of the dashboard easier to trust.

### Room 14: The Live Proof Strip

At the very bottom, there's a **strip of every money-pair Quidax offers, ticking in real time**. Every 15 seconds it asks the chalkboard for new numbers. There's a tiny counter next to it that says "last fetch 3s ago, 4s ago, 5s ago…" so you can literally watch it work.

If the counter keeps going past 15, that's me being honest: "Hey, the chalkboard is a little slow right now."

### Room 15: The Footer

At the bottom of the page is information about me, the person who built this. There's also some small print saying:

- "I built this on my own."
- "I don't work for Quidax."
- "This isn't financial advice."

That's the polite, careful stuff every honest website has at the end.

## How does the chalkboard get new numbers?

Glad you asked! Here's the secret.

### Step 1: The phone call

Every minute, my website **phones up Quidax** and says, "Hey, what are the latest prices?"

Quidax has its own giant computer that answers, with a long list of all the prices.

### Step 2: The double-check

When the prices come back, my website **doesn't trust them right away**. It checks every single number to make sure it looks real:

- Is it a number, not a word?
- Is it a positive number, not a negative one or zero?
- Does it make sense?

This is called **validation**. It's like a teacher checking your homework before putting a sticker on it.

### Step 3: The notebook

My website **writes the prices down in a notebook**. That way, if Quidax's giant computer takes a nap (computers do that sometimes), my website can just look in the notebook and say, "Well, here are the last prices I knew about — they're 30 seconds old, but they're real."

This notebook is called the **last-known-good cache**. It means the website never makes up fake numbers. If the real numbers aren't fresh, it tells you so.

### Step 4: The friendly bouncer

My website is **free for anyone in the world** to look at. But what if someone tries to be sneaky and ask for the prices a thousand times per second to crash my website?

There's a **bouncer at the door**. If anyone asks too fast, the bouncer says, "Slow down. Try again in a few seconds." This is called **rate limiting**. It keeps the website friendly for everyone.

### Step 5: The chalkboard updates

Every 15 seconds, the prices on the page tick to new values. You can literally watch them blink yellow when they change. That's how you know it's alive.

## Wait, how is this kept safe?

Good question! Here are the things I built in to keep the website safe:

1. **No passwords stored anywhere.** I don't ask you for your name, your email, your bank — nothing. So there's nothing to steal.
2. **No secret codes in the website.** Some apps have secret passwords written into them. Mine doesn't have any, because it doesn't need to.
3. **Security helmet (CSP).** The website tells web browsers, "Hey, only let pictures and styles come from these specific places — block everything else." If a bad person snuck a sneaky line of code in, the helmet would stop it.
4. **Locked windows (HSTS).** The website tells web browsers, "Always use the encrypted door (https), never the regular door (http)." Encrypted means even if a bad person was listening, they'd just hear gibberish.
5. **No iframes.** Bad guys sometimes try to put a real website inside their fake website, like a Russian doll. I told browsers "never let this website be put inside another website."
6. **Friendly bouncer.** Already explained — too many requests, no soup for you.

## Big words to remember

Here are some grown-up words I used. Now you can use them too and sound very smart.

- **Naira (₦):** Nigeria's money.
- **Dollar ($):** America's money, also used to buy things between countries.
- **Stablecoin:** A digital coin worth a fixed amount, usually one dollar (like USDT) or one naira (like cNGN).
- **Peg:** The fixed amount a stablecoin promises to be worth. When it breaks, it's called a **depeg**.
- **Spread / Premium:** The difference between two prices for the same thing.
- **NFEM:** The "Nigerian Foreign Exchange Market" — the price the Nigerian government says is the right one.
- **Parallel market:** The street price, sometimes higher than the official price.
- **Liquidity:** How easy it is to buy or sell a thing without changing its price. A puddle of water has low liquidity. The ocean has high liquidity.
- **TAM (Total Addressable Market):** How big the prize could be if everything went perfectly.
- **B2B:** Business-to-business. One company selling to another company instead of to a regular person.
- **API:** A way for one computer to talk to another. Like a vending machine — you push a button (a request), you get a soda (a response).
- **Cache:** A notebook a computer keeps so it doesn't have to ask the same question over and over.
- **Rate limit:** A speed limit, but for asking computers questions.
- **Latency:** How long something takes. Lower is better.
- **Provenance:** Where a piece of information came from. Like a museum label saying which painter made the painting.
- **Confidence:** How sure I am about something. "High confidence" means I'm pretty sure. "Low confidence" means I'm guessing more than measuring.
- **Counter-thesis:** A sentence that says "here's how I could be wrong." Brave people write these.
- **KPI:** A key number to track.
- **Recharts:** A computer drawing kit for making the graphs you see.
- **Next.js:** The set of computer tools I used to build the website. It's like a really good Lego set.
- **TypeScript:** The language I wrote the code in. It's like English but stricter, so the computer doesn't get confused.
- **Vercel:** The computer in the sky where my website actually lives. When you visit, it's Vercel that hands you the page.
- **GitHub:** The shared shelf where I keep all my code so I don't lose it. Lots of programmers use it.
